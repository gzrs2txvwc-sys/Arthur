In Taiwan, I was the American kid. In America, I was the Chinese kid. In Osaka, for the first time in my adult life, I was just a cook.

The kitchen doesn't negotiate identity in the same way. It negotiates heat and timing and whether the dashi is right. That suited me.

I came to Osaka six years ago on a working holiday visa after staging (the culinary term for interning) at a kaiseki restaurant in Kyoto for three months. The kaiseki stage was humbling in a way that formal culinary training had not been — not because the techniques were entirely foreign, but because the *philosophy* was. Japanese cuisine at its formal level is about restraint, about what you don't add, about revealing the ingredient rather than transforming it. I had been trained in the French tradition, which is almost the opposite.

After the stage, I moved to Osaka. The food culture there was what I'd come for: democratic, abundant, honest, and completely serious about flavor without being precious about form.

## Getting a kitchen job as a foreigner

Let me be honest about the process, because the romanticized version is unhelpful.

Getting a restaurant job in Japan as a foreign national without Japanese language ability is difficult, not impossible, and depends heavily on sector and city. Osaka's food industry is slightly more open than Tokyo's because Osaka has a larger merchant culture tradition and a different relationship to outsiders — more pragmatic, less formal.

My Japanese when I arrived was functional but not fluent. I could communicate in the kitchen, but a full job interview in Japanese was beyond me for the first year. I spent six months working at a tourist-adjacent restaurant in Namba that served an international menu, learning kitchen Japanese and building a work history. This was not glamorous. It was necessary.

The kitchen vocabulary is learnable in about three months if you're already a cook: the terms for preparation methods, timing calls, ordering from the floor. What takes longer is the *politeness register* — Japanese kitchens have a formal hierarchy expressed through language, and getting the formality levels right with senior cooks matters more than most Western kitchens I'd worked in.

After a year, I began approaching more serious Japanese restaurants. My break came through a connection at a food festival — another cook who had worked with a French-Japanese fusion chef who was looking for someone with Western training. I started there as a line cook. I'm now sous chef.

## The housing rejection

I want to talk about this because it affected me and it affects most foreigners and nobody admits how much it stings.

I was rejected by three apartments before finding mine. The first rejection came through an agency with no explanation offered, which I later understood meant the landlord had declined because of my foreign status. The second and third were explicit: the agencies told me upfront that the landlord only accepted Japanese tenants.

The fourth was my place — a 1LDK in Namba-dori, second floor, small kitchen, close to everything. The landlord was a man in his seventies who had previously rented to a Brazilian family and had decided that foreign tenants were acceptable. He used a guarantor service. He apologized to me directly for the difficulty I had encountered. Then he took me out for kushikatsu.

I have not moved since. I renewed four times. He drops by every six months, ostensibly to check the apartment, actually to ask if I have any good restaurant recommendations.

## What Osaka taught me about feeding people

The phrase in Osaka is *kuidaore* — "eat until you drop." It sounds like excess. It isn't. It's a philosophy.

In the kitchens I've worked in here, the philosophy is not about luxury ingredients or technical complexity (though Osaka has both). It is about *intent*. You are trying to give someone something that they will actually enjoy. The measure is not elegance; the measure is whether they finish the bowl.

This sounds obvious. It isn't obvious if you've been trained in environments where the measure is plating or technique or whether the critic approves. Osaka kitchens care about the person eating. That reorientation changed how I cook.

The best meal I've had in six years here was not at a Michelin-starred restaurant. It was a bowl of *horumon-yaki* at a standing restaurant in Shinsekai at 11pm on a rainy Tuesday, eaten next to a retired truck driver who was explaining to me why his preferred cut was better than mine. He was right. I changed my order. We talked for an hour.

That is Osaka food culture: generous, opinionated, and completely unbothered by formality.

---

## What six years means

I came for a year. The year became two, became four, became six.

I have a residency card now, not a work visa. I have a neighborhood where people know my face. I have the kind of Japanese that works in normal life and almost works in difficult situations. I still get things wrong. I still occasionally bow when a handshake was expected. I still find the housing bureaucracy Byzantine and occasionally unfair.

But Osaka accommodated me. Not enthusiastically, not without friction, but genuinely. The city made room. I stayed.

If you're thinking about working in food here: come. Adjust your expectations about hierarchy — they're real, and worth understanding. Learn kitchen Japanese before you arrive. Be patient with the bureaucracy. Find a guarantor service on day one.

And eat standing up when the option is there. It tastes better that way.
