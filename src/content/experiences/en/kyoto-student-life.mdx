My research is about urban memory and historical preservation. I chose Kyoto for obvious professional reasons. I stayed for reasons I still can't fully explain.

I enrolled at Kyoto University's Graduate School of Letters in a joint research program — my German university had a partnership that made the bureaucracy manageable, though "manageable" is doing significant work in that sentence. The paperwork took four months. The visa category required a letter from my German supervisor, a letter from my Japanese supervisor, proof of funding, a research plan in both German and Japanese, and a degree of faith that all of this would eventually resolve itself. It did.

---

## What nobody told me about Kyoto University

The campus is extraordinary and confusing in equal measure. Kyoto University has the most Nobel laureates of any Asian university and a culture to match — deeply serious, historically aware of its own prestige, and organized around the assumption that researchers are adults who can manage their own time.

That last part was a significant adjustment from German academic culture, which is highly structured. Here, I met with my Japanese supervisor once a month. He gave me access to the library and a desk in the graduate student room and essentially said: do your research. For six months I felt I was failing. Eventually I understood I was learning to be a researcher rather than a student.

The graduate student community is a mix of Japanese students (deeply hardworking, generally reserved, warming slowly) and international students (more immediately open, bonded by shared bewilderment). The international student association runs events but they tend toward the formal; the real connections happen informally, in the coffee shop near the north gate where everyone goes between 10am and noon.

---

## Language: the slow emergency

My Japanese when I arrived was JLPT N5 — enough to introduce myself and order from a picture menu. Kyoto University's Graduate School of Letters operates primarily in Japanese. My supervisor spoke excellent English; most seminars did not.

The first three months I attended seminars and understood approximately 40% of what was said. I took notes in English and German, looked things up afterward, and smiled in a way that I hoped communicated thoughtfulness rather than complete incomprehension.

I enrolled in Kyoto University's Japanese language program for foreign researchers — intensive, practical, and free for registered students. I also hired a private tutor from HelloTalk for conversation practice twice a week. By month six, I could participate in seminars at a basic level. By month twelve, I could argue.

The thing nobody says about learning Japanese in Kyoto specifically: it is harder than Tokyo, and also better. Kyoto dialect (*Kyō-kotoba*) is softer and more formal than standard Japanese, and Kyoto people notice when you use it and appreciate the attempt. It is also genuinely beautiful. Standard Japanese in Kyoto sounds slightly wrong to the ear, like wearing a business suit to a tea ceremony.

## The rhythm of a Kyoto life

Living in Kyoto recalibrates your sense of time. The city operates on different rhythms than any European or North American city I have known.

There is a seasonal consciousness here that is not sentimental but practical. The city prepares for cherry blossom season, for summer festivals, for autumn leaves, for winter silence. These are not tourist events — or they are tourist events, but they are also genuinely structuring to daily life in a way that I began to understand only after several complete cycles. By my second autumn, I found myself planning around *momiji* season the way I plan around German Advent at home.

My neighborhood, Sakyo-ku, north of the university, was quiet enough to work and close enough to everything. I cycled everywhere. The cycling infrastructure is not Amsterdam, but the distances are short and the streets in the northern neighborhoods are peaceful enough that it becomes meditative. I worked from my apartment or from a tea shop called Nakagawa Masashichi, which serves matcha and has no wifi, which is either a problem or a feature depending on your discipline.

---

## What a city that remembers everything does to you

This is the part I struggle to explain to people who haven't lived here.

Kyoto has 1,700 years of continuous habitation. There are temples that have been in continuous operation for eleven centuries. The city has been destroyed and rebuilt and refined over a time span that makes German history feel recent. When you live inside that, something happens to your relationship with permanence.

I became more patient. Not as a personality trait but as a practical adjustment to a city that takes the long view. Bureaucracy that would frustrate me in Berlin felt comprehensible in Kyoto — not because it was less bureaucratic, but because I had started to understand that careful, slow processes had built the thing I was living inside.

My research is better for it. I understand now what it means for a city to successfully transmit its past to the present without becoming a museum. Kyoto knows how to be alive and old at the same time. That is a rare thing.

I will go home eventually. I do not know when.
