I arrived at Narita on a Tuesday in October with a TEFL certificate, a suitcase, and the belief that Japan would be an adventure. It was. But not the adventure I expected.

The first six months were the postcard version. Cherry blossoms, vending machines, the polite bewilderment of being somewhere that feels fundamentally ordered when you have spent your life in chaos. My students were eager. My apartment was tiny and perfect. I felt, for the first time in my adult life, like I was in the right place.

Then came the cultural wall.

---

## The job nobody warns you about

I was teaching at an *eikaiwa* — a private English conversation school — in Shinjuku. The work itself was fine: adults who wanted to practice conversational English, mostly for business travel or personal satisfaction. But the job structure was something I hadn't encountered before.

In Canada, if there's a problem at work, you raise it. You email your supervisor. You have a conversation. In my *eikaiwa*, problems were handled through a system of silent adjustment. If a student complained about a lesson style, I would find a new scheduling template in my inbox the next morning with no explanation. The feedback was built into the architecture, invisible and impossible to respond to directly.

It took me eight months to understand this was not passive aggression. It was *consideration*. My supervisors were trying to save me the embarrassment of a direct correction. Once I understood that, everything became easier. I stopped looking for explanations and started looking for what had changed.

---

## The housing scramble

Finding my apartment was the hardest thing I did in Japan.

Foreign applicants face what the community calls the "gaijin wall": landlords who refuse foreign tenants outright, or impose additional deposit requirements, or require guarantors that foreigners don't have. My school offered a relocation service that found me a place in a gaijin house (a shared house for international residents) for the first three months, which helped. But transitioning to a real apartment was brutal.

I was rejected by four apartments before an agency found me a landlord in Shinjuku-ku who had previously rented to foreigners and was willing to use a guarantor service. The guarantor service cost one month's rent. Worth every yen.

**Practical advice**: Start your apartment search through agencies that specialize in foreign applicants (Sakura House, Tokyo Rent, Oak House). Use a guarantor service rather than asking a Japanese colleague, which puts them in an awkward position. Budget for key money and deposit — typically 2-4 months' rent upfront.

## The loneliness nobody posts about

Year two was when things got real.

Year one is adrenaline. Everything is new, every experience is content, the strangeness is the whole point. Year two, the strangeness becomes the background, and you start to feel its weight.

I didn't have a Japanese social circle. My colleagues were friendly but bounded by professional propriety. The foreigners I knew from the first year had rotated — teaching jobs turn over quickly. And my Japanese was at a level where I could function but not connect: good enough to buy groceries, not good enough to explain how I was really doing.

What helped was Shimokitazawa. On weekends, I would take the Odakyū line to that neighborhood and spend the day in its record shops and coffee shops and live music venues. It was full of artists and students and people who had also, in some way, not quite fit the main story. I didn't have deep friendships there. But I had the feeling of being in a place that had made room for odd people, and that was enough.

## What I know now

Three years in, I speak functional Japanese, I have a small real social circle, and I feel something close to at home. The things I wish I'd known at the start:

**On work**: The *eikaiwa* industry is a real job with real career concerns. Understand your visa terms. Keep your CoE documents organized. Know the difference between a dispatch contract and a direct hire. The difference matters enormously if things go wrong.

**On language**: Start immediately. Not an app, not a class — a tutor, once a week, Italki or HelloTalk. Real conversation practice from week one. Your Japanese will plateau until you're willing to be embarrassed.

**On community**: The foreigner community in Tokyo is large and fragmented. Find your niche early. For me, it was music. For others, it's hiking groups, climbing gyms, food communities. Tokyo has everything — but you have to find your version of it.

**On patience**: Japan will make you feel like you're doing everything wrong before it makes you feel like you've earned your place. That earning is real. Keep going.
