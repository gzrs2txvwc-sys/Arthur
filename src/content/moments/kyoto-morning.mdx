Before the crowds arrive, the thousand torii gates are yours alone.

I had set an alarm for 5:15am — a violent hour, the alarm going off in my ryokan room like an accusation. But I had read somewhere that Fushimi Inari at first light was worth any sacrifice of sleep, and so I pulled on my clothes in the dark, quietly, the way you move when you don't want to wake a city.

The walk from the station was twenty minutes through streets that had not yet decided what they were. A bakery exhaled warm bread smell through a vent. Two crows argued in a pine tree. A woman in a housedress moved a broom across a concrete apron with the careful attention of someone doing something sacred.

---

The first torii gate arrives suddenly — that particular red, the color they call *vermilion* in translation but which in reality is something warmer, more alive. It is the color of the inside of a pomegranate, of a warning that does not feel dangerous.

Then the second. Then the third. And quickly, without fanfare, you are inside the corridor of them, and the light changes.

This is the thing no photograph captures: the light inside the torii corridor at 6am is not the same light as outside it. It is filtered through centuries of lacquer and intention, bent and reddened, and it falls across the mossy path in bands that make you feel, genuinely feel, that you have entered a different arrangement of space.

The mountain breathes. The foxes have just gone back wherever foxes go.

---

I climbed for an hour. Alone almost the entire way. A man passed me going down — a local, I thought, wearing ordinary clothes and earbuds — and for a moment I felt the strangeness of this: that this extraordinary place was ordinary to someone. That someone had walked these gates a hundred times and still walked them again, unremarkably, at 6am on a Thursday.

That, I think, is what I was really looking for. Not the tourist experience of a sacred place, but evidence that the sacred can be routine. That it does not exhaust itself by being visited every day. That the foxes do not resent us.

At the summit, I sat on a stone and looked out over Kyoto while it was still mostly dark. The city below was a spread of amber dots, patient and quiet, waiting for the sun to tell it to begin.

I thought: this is what it means to feel old in a good way. Not aged. *Deepened.*

The light arrived slowly, the way important things do.
